## COLD OPEN — the receipts [docs: MH 2019 article, MH 2025 article]
In December 2019, a fitness magazine ran a rumor about a 26-year-old actor almost nobody had heard of. The headline said Warner Brothers was eyeing him to replace Henry Cavill as Superman. Four months later, that same actor told an interviewer exactly what he thought of the role: Superman is under-appreciated and over-simplified. And when the interviewer said he could picture him in the cape, he joked: great to know. I'll take that in writing. Maybe we can start a petition. Three years later, the phone rang. And the man on the other end said: Hey David, it's James Gunn. This is how David Corenswet turned a hundred-and-ninety-five-pound frame into Superman — forty pounds in a matter of months, documented by the people who trained him, fed him, and measured him for a suit he'd outgrow before it was finished.

## PART 1 — THE THEATER KID
Start in Philadelphia, 1993. A high school drama kid at a Quaker school, with a teacher who refused to let him coast. He remembered it years later, quote: don't let that be an excuse not to push yourself and not to work harder than everybody else. That teacher was the last person he personally told he'd be Superman. From there, Juilliard, class of 2016, and its famously brutal movement classes — training he says was, quote, super high intensity almost to the point of being impossible. Remember that, because it comes back when they hang him from wires. The problem was the frame. Six-foot-four, and by his own account, about a hundred and ninety-five pounds. In his words: which is not very much for somebody who's six-four. Netflix's The Politician made him a face in 2019. The Superman rumor followed within months. He'd already called the part his pie-in-the-sky ambition.

## PART 2 — TWO SECRETS
June 2023. Warner Brothers calls him in for a final screen test — three Clark Kents, three Lois Lanes, suit-up on day two. Two days after he found out about the test, his wife Julia told him she was pregnant. Two enormous secrets, and he could tell no one about either. He said, quote: we had these two quite huge secrets that we just had between the two of us. And that was intense. Then, while he was home on a break from filming Twisters, an unknown number: Hey David, it's James Gunn. His answer: can you prove that? The offer came with a brief. Quote: you're in good shape, but I want you to get a trainer. I want you to work on your shoulders and your vulnerability. And one more detail that matters for everything that follows: the costume department built his suit from the screen-test measurements. Six-foot-four. A hundred and ninety-five pounds.

## PART 3 — THE LONELY MATH [docs: GQ Mascitti article]
Gunn hand-picked the trainer: Paolo Mascitti, the Hollywood coach whose work on The Suicide Squad Gunn had loved. The program started in July 2023, and none of it was exotic. Push, pull, legs. Five to six days a week. Presses, pull-ups, pull-downs, rows — in Mascitti's words, all the big ones — with progressive overload as the entire religion: track the reps, track the weight, advance every week. Most sets in the six-to-twelve range, isolation work pushed to fifteen or twenty to finish the muscle off. The published push day looks like this: incline dumbbell press. Bench press supersetted with cable flies. Seated shoulder press supersetted with lateral raises. Triceps extensions supersetted with curls. Four sets of ten, last set to failure. On top of it, a lot of core work — because this body had a job: harness stunts. He credits those impossible Juilliard movement classes for surviving the wires, strung up, in his words, by my underpants, holding every limb in one steady, exact position. His one weakness, per his trainer: walking lunges. He's great at squats. He just hates lunges. He did them anyway, with jazz in his headphones. And his own description of the work is the best training quote of the year. Quote: there is a certain loneliness that comes at the gym. In the moment when you're going for that ninth rep, or you've put a little extra weight on the bar, nobody's going to do it for you. It's just you, alone in your mind, seeing if you can go a little bit further than you thought you could.

## PART 4 — THE FUEL
The eating was the harder job. Corenswet's own number: I was aiming for about four thousand five hundred calories a day. His trainer has put the peak even higher — around six thousand, by Mascitti's telling — before cutting to roughly thirty-five hundred as cameras approached, to trade a little mass for definition. The structure underneath was strict: fifty percent carbs, thirty protein, twenty fat. Around seven meals and snacks a day. Breakfast alone: six egg whites plus two whole eggs, up to two cups of oatmeal, berries, Greek yogurt with almonds, and a protein shake. Then rice, beef, chicken, fish and vegetables on repeat, with the carbs stacked before training. Mostly whole, unprocessed food — the quality of the calories mattered as much as the count. And one documented vice. His trainer's words: he loves cereal. That's his kryptonite. He'd be on a check-in call, eating cereal, asking, what's wrong with cereal? The third pillar was non-negotiable. Sleep. Mascitti again: everything is part of the puzzle. You take one of the things out, and it's not going to happen.

## PART 5 — THE DOUBLE FORTY
Here's the part no other superhero transformation has. While David was gaining his forty pounds, his wife was pregnant with their first child — and gained about forty pounds in the same months. His words: she gained about forty pounds while she was pregnant, and in the same period I gained about forty pounds for Superman. I started snoring, and had to use her pregnancy pillow. And the proof of the transformation wasn't a gym selfie. It was a tape measure. Four to five months after the screen test, he walked into his first costume fitting — into a suit built for a hundred-ninety-five-pound man — at two hundred and forty pounds. His words: that first fitting was a bit of a squeeze.

## PART 6 — SUPERMAN WITH A BRUISE
Filming began in early 2024. Training dropped to three or four sessions a week — still two hours at a time, half of them coached over FaceTime between setups. The stunts were real enough to leave marks: the huge flying leap you've seen in the trailer is also the take that ended with a very personal bruise, a story he told on a lie detector, because, in his words, I couldn't lie about that. It's on film. On July 11th, 2025, Superman opened to roughly two hundred and twenty million dollars worldwide. The 2019 rumor had become a fact. The 2020 petition joke had become a prophecy. And the physique that carried it was never meant to be a bodybuilder's. The design brief, per his trainer: he needed to look more like a farm boy than a complete bodybuilder.

## PART 7 — DO IT AGAIN
The sequel, Man of Tomorrow, was announced within two months of the opening. Cameras rolled again in April 2026, in Georgia, for a July 2027 release. Which means somewhere right now, the lonely math is running again: the plates, the meals, the sleep, the ninth rep nobody can do for you.

## THE PLAN — HIS RULES
You don't need a Warner Brothers contract. You need the rules his team actually used. Rule one: train for the role, not the mirror. Define the brief first — his was shoulders, and vulnerability. Rule two: push, pull, legs, forever. Big compound lifts, six to twelve reps, and progressive overload you actually write down. Rule three: master the lift, not the show. His trainer's doctrine: full range of motion, own the negative, add weight or reps every week. Rule four: eat like it's your job. Real food, protein at every meal, carbs before training, and enough total calories that the scale has no choice. Rule five: sleep is the third pillar — take one piece out and the puzzle doesn't work. And rule six: keep your kryptonite. His was cereal. He kept it, and it kept him sane.
